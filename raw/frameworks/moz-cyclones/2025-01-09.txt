Mozambique - Cyclones
As of 9 January 2025

1. Executive Summary
This document presents the framework for anticipatory action (AA) and early response (ER) in Mozambique, including the
trigger, the pre-agreed action plan and the pre-arranged financing.
The context: Mozambique is regularly impacted by cyclones, especially in the low-lying coastal areas that are home to a
majority of the population. Since 2017 alone, some 5 million people have been affected, mostly in the provinces of
Zambezia, Nampula and Sofala. Moreover, cyclones are the natural hazard with the greatest attributable economic losses.
Since 2000, Mozambique has been impacted by a major tropical cyclone every other year on average. The cyclones that
hit the Mozambican coast are increasing in number and intensity 1.
The objective: This framework seeks to provide coordinated anticipatory action and early response to people at risk of or
affected by cyclones in Mozambique. The initiative provides life-saving assistance to people across six provinces: Cabo
Delgado, Nampula, Zambezia, Sofala, Inhambane, and Gaza, with AA being implemented in selected pilot districts.
The development process: The framework was developed through a participatory process led by the Resident and
Humanitarian Coordinator and coordinated by OCHA Mozambique in collaboration with the national technical working group
on anticipatory action.

The financing and activities included in this framework will be triggered by either the following readiness trigger, action
trigger or observational trigger.

The financing
The Emergency Relief Coordinator (ERC) has pre-arranged up to $6 million from the Central Emergency Response Fund
(CERF) to support the present framework. The funding will allow partners to provide assistance in the Food Security
($1.58M), WASH ($1.3M), Shelter ($1.25M), Health ($700k), Sexual and Reproductive Health ($364k), CCCM ($350k),
GBV ($336k), Child Protection ($100k), and Logistics ($20k) sectors and areas of responsibilities. In addition, based on the
given activation scenario, WFP commits up to USD 979,000 from its dedicated anticipatory action trust fund for the
2024/2025 season. Funding tranches for readiness, anticipatory action and early response are tied to the different triggers.
The learning
The present framework offers important learning opportunities. Partners have agreed to conduct a joint post-distribution
monitoring exercise in the case of a framework activation. In addition, an after-action review will be conducted to gather
lessons learned from the development and potential implementation of the framework. Partners also commit to engage in
more comprehensive learning efforts in the future.

2. Introduction
Objectives of anticipatory action
There is broad agreement for the international humanitarian sector to move from a largely responsive to a more anticipatory
approach. An anticipatory approach leads to a more effective, efficient and dignified response. It also protects hard-won
development gains.
Today, we can predict with increasing confidence the occurrence and humanitarian impact of certain climate-related shocks
and infectious disease outbreaks. By combining different analytical approaches, out-of-the-ordinary weather events can not
only be predicted, but their projected impact can proactively be mitigated based on pre-identified anticipatory actions.
Building on growing evidence that acting prior to the onset of a predictable hazard is significantly more (cost-)effective than
traditional humanitarian response, OCHA has facilitated the setup of multiple Anticipatory Action frameworks.
Each framework comprises 3 core elements, all of which are underpinned by a clear learning, monitoring and evaluation
plan:
•
•
•

A robust forecasting embedded in a clear decision-making process (the trigger).
Pre-agreed activities that can fundamentally alter the trajectory of the crisis (the action plan).
Pre-arranged funding (the financing).

Anticipatory action is still an innovative space. Thus, in addition to the 3 core elements, OCHA also invests in documenting
evidence and learning from each framework.
Each anticipatory action framework is tailored to the local context. Additional core principles that apply to the framework in
Mozambique can be found in the annex.

Early response provisions
There is growing evidence demonstrating the positive impact of anticipatory action on communities affected by predictable
shocks2. At the same time, the erratic nature of cyclones, which often rapidly intensify or change direction unexpectedly
creates operational challenges. In such cases, a cyclone may cause significant humanitarian consequences, but
anticipatory action may not be possible or to a limited extent. Lessons from existing anticipatory action initiatives for cyclones
have shown that including provisions for early response in the framework can facilitate (when deemed necessary) a much
faster mobilization of pre-agreed activities and processes to respond effectively to the most immediate post-disaster needs.
As such, by including an early response component, the present framework pursues the following objectives:
•
•
•
•

Ensure a timely response that supports communities and addresses their specific needs before, during and in the
immediate aftermath of the shock
Allow for the release of funds and quick implementation even when the window of opportunity for anticipatory
action has passed
Ensure greater geographical flexibility
Capture events characterized by severe rainfall (see 3. Forecast and Trigger)

Exposure to and humanitarian impact of cyclones in Mozambique
Mozambique is regularly impacted by cyclones, especially in the low-lying coastal areas that are home to a majority of the
population. Since 2017 alone, some 5 million people have been affected, mostly in the provinces of Zambezia, Nampula
and Sofala. Moreover, cyclones are the natural hazard with the greatest attributable economic losses. Tropical Cyclone
Idai, which hit Mozambique in 2019, is, for instance, one of the two most costly events (estimated at US$ 1.96 billion) in
Africa over the past 50 years. The annual cyclone season in Mozambique usually lasts from November until March.
However, events may also occur outside of this period. Historical data from the National Institute for Disaster Management
and Risk Reduction (Instituto Nacional de Gestão e Redução do Risco de Desastres, INGD) shows that the impact of
cyclones in Mozambique has varied, affecting between 73,000 people (Cyclone Chalane in 2020) and 1.5 million people
(Cyclone Idai in 2019). Women and girls were among the most vulnerable groups during and after the disaster. They faced
increased risks of gender-based violence (GBV), displacement, and loss of livelihoods. Women, particularly in rural areas,
were also heavily affected due to challenges in accessing relief and recovery resources. In 2023, Tropical Cyclone Freddy
hit Mozambique twice, causing destructive winds, extreme rainfall, and widespread flooding. Despite accurate early
warnings and coordinated disaster management, significant damage occurred, affecting infrastructure, displacing
populations, and disrupting essential services. Roads were cut off, hindering social services, trade and provision of
healthcare thus worsening the cholera outbreak that had started in September 2022. In 2024, Cyclone Chido affected over
450,000 people in Cabo Delgado and Nampula. The cyclones that hit the Mozambican coast are increasing in number and
intensity3. Since 2000, Mozambique has been impacted by a major tropical cyclone every other year on average (see figure
below).

The National Institute for Disaster Risk Management and Reduction (INGD) of Mozambique, in collaboration with partners,
has developed a Manual of Standard Operating Procedures for Anticipatory Action to Floods and Cyclones building on the
country’s experience with developing a similar manual for anticipatory action for drought in 2022. The AA component of the
present framework is in complete alignment with the manual and was developed in collaboration with the national technical
working (TWG) group for AA. Specifically, this applies to the following elements:
•
•
•

The action trigger and activation protocol
The anticipatory action plan
The geographical prioritization

In agreement with the government-led TWG, the early response component will complement the anticipatory action efforts
without formally constituting a part of the national manual for AA.
Furthermore, Mozambique’s National Adaptation Plan (NAP) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate
Change (UNFCCC) identifies the increased frequency and intensity of tropical cyclones as a significant threat. Anticipatory
action, including through this coordinated framework, aims to also reduce vulnerability and enhance the adaptive capacity
of its communities in the face of climate change.

3. Forecast and Trigger
Forecasts
Under the World Meteorological Organization’s Tropical Cyclone Programme, the Regional Specialized Meteorological
Centre (RSMC) in La Réunion has the responsibility for detecting and forecasting tropical cyclones (TCs) in its zone of
responsibility, which includes the South-West Indian Ocean basin, encompassing Mozambique. As such, RSMC La
Réunion’s TC forecasts are the internationally recognized authoritative source for TCs in this region. RSMC La Réunion is
operated by Météo-France.
RSMC La Réunion issues official TC track forecasts with a 120-hour lead time. These forecasts include the TC’s forecasted
category and position and 5-day TC formation risk outlooks. This framework includes an observational component that uses
the forecasts issued around landfall and rainfall data from NASA’s Integrated Multi-satellitE Retrievals for GPM (IMERG)4
on the days before, of and after landfall.

Triggers and thresholds
The financing and activities included in this framework will be triggered by either the readiness trigger, action trigger or the
observational trigger. The activation thresholds and data sources have been selected to ensure full integration with the
government-led AA activities and to capture most of the historical rapid response allocations made by CERF while
maintaining a return period for activation around 3 years. The framework relies on the following triggers to release prearranged financing and implement activities.
•

•

•

Readiness trigger (lead time up to 120 hours before landfall):
o The readiness trigger is using the same conditions defined for the action trigger (see below) but is
considering forecasts with lead times between 72 and 120 hours
Action trigger (lead time between 72 and 6 hours before landfall) (see Table 1):
o The exposed area corresponding to a severe tropical storm of at least 89km/h 5 overlaps with at least one
of these districts: Angoche, Maganja Da Costa, Machanga or Govuro OR
o The exposed area corresponding to a tropical cyclone of at least 119km/h overlaps with at least one of
these districts: Mogincual, Namacurra, Dondo, Cidade Da Beira, Buzi or Vilankulo.
Observational trigger (from landfall up to 48 hours after landfall):
o Forecasts issued around landfall indicate that landfall will be within the selected provinces and the intensity
at landfall will be at least a tropical cyclone of 119km/h OR
o The sum of rainfall within a given province the days before, of and after landfall (sum of rainfall over 3 days)
around the landfall location exceeds 120 mm.
o If the observational rainfall trigger is reached only, either the HC or the CERF secretariat can stop an
activation within 48 hours of landfall, should they determine that available information indicates that the
humanitarian impact is not severe to an extent that justifies a CERF allocation.

Partners also agreed to conform with the government-mandated cut-off time of 6 hours before landfall for the action trigger
to protect humanitarian personnel and people targeted. This means, if an action trigger is reached at any time during the 6
hours before expected landfall, the framework will not be activated, and no activities will be implemented.
In collaboration with technical partners, the analysis of the forecast has been standardized to consider both the cyclone
track uncertainty and the expected extent of the wind buffers for the different thresholds in the calculation of the projected
cyclone impact area. This analysis code is publicly available in this repository 6.

Roles and responsibilities in monitoring the trigger
The monitoring process is managed by OCHA’s Centre for Humanitarian Data (CHD) in collaboration with technical partners
from partner agencies.
During the monitoring period, every 6 hours, the forecast released by RSMC in La Réunion is evaluated to determine
whether the trigger has been reached. The process is outlined below:
•
•

The forecast, containing up to 120-hour lead time, is produced by the RSMC Centre.
It is automatically added to an FTP server7 managed by the RSMC. From the FTP server:
o Forecast files are downloaded and processed by a script running on GitHub.
o The forecast is analyzed for wind speeds and rainfall thresholds that overlap with predefined geographic
zones.
o Data is validated against thresholds for severe tropical storms (89 km/h) and tropical cyclones (at least 119
km/h) for designated districts.
▪ If a trigger threshold is met, the monitoring team emails the relevant parties under the activation
protocol (draft activation messages can be found in the Annex)
▪ If no trigger is reached, the system monitors the next forecast cycle and emails the CHD’s data
science team.

Monitoring will occur from 1 October to 31 May. If seasonal forecasts indicate a significant risk of events outside of these
months, the monitoring period may be extended.

Activation protocol
Pre-defining this activation protocol is critical in order to avoid delays, miscommunication, uncertainty or errors once the
trigger threshold is reached. OCHA’s Centre for Humanitarian Data will monitor the triggers. Activation of the action trigger
will be formally decided by the government’s Technical Council on Disaster Management (Conselho Técnico de Gestão e
Redução do Risco de Desastres, CTGD). The readiness and observational triggers, which are not currently included in the
national AA manual, will be initiated by OCHA. Additionally, informal communication channels will be maintained to ensure
effective information sharing.

4. Anticipatory Action and Early Response Plan
Selection criteria
Anticipatory actions aim to protect populations most at risk of being impacted by a shock. The following criteria were applied
to select actions:
1. Anticipatory character: Is the action effective in preventing or reducing the humanitarian impact of the shock?
2. Ability to deliver within the window of opportunity: Is it possible to carry out the action effectively with the
available forecast lead time, i.e. in the window of opportunity between the trigger and the cyclone making landfall?
3. Operational capacity: Do the agency and its implementing partners have the demonstrated institutional capacity
(thematic, logistic, administrative, financial, human resources) to implement the action effectively given the lead
time and scale?
4. No regrets approach: In the case of a false alarm, will the proposed actions benefit rather than negatively affect
the targeted population?
Early response activities were selected based on experience from previous shocks, operational capacity and the ability to
deliver within a short period of time after the shock manifests. The implementation of the early response activities as part
of this framework should not exceed 2-4 weeks after landfall.

Priority interventions
The pre-agreed actions, identified in collaboration with the national technical working group on anticipatory action, will
focus on the following core objectives:
•

•
•
•

Communicating risks, engaging with communities, and early warning messaging: Partners will collaborate
to disseminate joint early warning signals as well as sector-specific messaging, including on people’s rights to safe
and free assistance. All partners will harmonize their messages through national government platforms in addition
to agency-specific channels. Early Warning messages will be distributed after the action trigger in line with the
national anticipatory action standard operating procedures.
Supporting people in temporary accommodation centers (TACs): Partners will ensure TACs are prepared, and
people receive food, WASH and other assistance.
Providing in-kind assistance: A set of in-kind assistance activities such as the provision of shelter kits, dignity
kits and hygiene and water treatment products.
Facilitating access to services: The framework supports access to health, gender-based violence (GBV), child
protection, and water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) services.

Targeting
Partners will jointly target temporary accommodation centers identified in coordination with the government. Agencies will
collaborate to identify households, including women-headed households, receiving joint assistance as a preparedness
activity. After cyclone landfall, partners will coordinate their activities, including targeting, with relevant stakeholders,
including the government, civil society organizations and through OCHA-facilitated coordination mechanisms.

Beneficiary registration
While a significant portion of the in-kind assistance and services will be provided in temporary accommodation centers, the
only way of ensuring that sector-specific cash and certain in-kind assistance can be distributed to those in need in the very
short window of opportunity prior to cyclone landfall is by pre-registering households and individuals in advance.
Registration processes must be inclusive and accessible to women and girls and other vulnerable groups, including people
with disabilities and those in rural or hard-to-reach areas.

IOM
While the Government conducts registration, IOM through displacement tracking matrix (DTM) and camp coordination and
camp management (CCCM) provides technical assistance, tools, and resources to enhance efficiency, inclusiveness and
coverage.
UNFPA
While working closely with its national partners, as well as with the government, UNFPA will identify and register vulnerable
women and girls in high-risk areas who might require support during or after a cyclone. Pre-crisis vulnerability data, including
information on pregnant women, and displaced people, will also be utilized. Additionally, GBV Safety Audits will be
conducted to further assess risks and identify vulnerable women and girls. Besides, the Mobile sexual and reproductive
health (SRH) Team will conduct real-time registration of target groups in need of SRH support.
UNICEF
UNICEF’s services are a mix of community and household level beneficiaries. For community services, for example,
deployment of mobile health teams, or provision of health kits to the district authorities, no beneficiary registration is required
ahead of time. The Ministry of Health records user data so UNICEF can understand the number of users, visit purposes,
etc. Similarly, mental health and psychosocial support services (MHPSS), water trucking, water and sanitation in institutions
and water infrastructure do not require beneficiary registration; services are available for those who need them. For
distribution of health kits, this will be prioritized for those staying in accommodation centers. Registration will be coordinated
with the government and center officials. Lastly, messaging will be done through community radios, mobile teams or other
means and do not require registration.
WFP
The successful implementation of WFP’s anticipatory action activities does not require pre-registration of beneficiaries since
there is no provision of sector-specific cash or other forms of in-kind assistance outside of temporary accommodation
centers. Ahead of the season, as part of its general preparedness measures, WFP regularly updates a list of community
leaders who are an important partner in the dissemination of early warning messages. WFP confirmed that ahead of the
2024/25 season, this step was completed.

Ensuring preparedness for a timely implementation
Anticipatory action and early response require a significant level of preparedness to be able to respond quickly and
effectively before a shock occurs and during its early stages. This is especially true for sudden-onset shocks. As a result,
partners have made investments to enhance their operational readiness and be able to implement their activities without
delay.
IOM
Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM)
DTM enables decision makers and responders to provide populations with better context specific assistance. As part of this
framework, IOM will pre-deploy planning, pre-position data collection tools, and deploy enumerators as early response.
Deployment planning includes pre-identifying enumerators and data collection teams for immediate deployment and
establishing clear communication and reporting structures with the government, internally, and among partners to enable
rapid data flow and analysis. Pre-positioning efforts involve updating data collection tools tailored to anticipated disaster
impacts, providing these tools and resources to enumerators and partners in targeted areas, and offering training to
enumerators and government counterparts. During early response, enumerators are deployed to affected areas to collect
preliminary data on population counts and urgent needs. This data is promptly shared with the government, partners, and
sectors to inform early response decision-making.
Camp Coordination and Camp Management (CCCM)
CCCM ensures effective coordination and support through information dissemination, deployment of teams, site

Shelter
The Shelter programme targets aid effectively through beneficiary assessments, cash disbursement coordination, and

on timelines, processes, and conditions, supported by feedback mechanisms for accountability. Household needs for shelter
NFIs are rapidly assessed in coordination with local authorities, prioritizing vulnerable groups. Pre-positioned NFIs, including
tarpaulins and repair kits, are mobilized to high-risk districts. Accessible distribution points are established with local
authorities to ensure equitable access. Distributions prioritize at-risk populations and include demonstrations to enhance
the proper use of items, maximizing their impact.
UNFPA
As part of its preparedness efforts, UNFPA prepositions emergency supplies in high-risk areas, including emergency tents
to ensure the continuity of Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) services and care, as well as dignity kits. Through its
leadership of the Gender-Based Violence Area of Responsibility (GBV) AoR, UNFPA continues to strengthen coordination
mechanisms with government agencies, local partners, and humanitarian clusters.
UNICEF
UNICEF maintains partnerships with a variety of government and NGO (both local and international) partners and has
agreements for procurement and transport of goods. Through these agreements, UNICEF is able to quickly jump-start
activities to allow for anticipatory actions to be completed.
WFP
For the dissemination of early warning messages, WFP has worked with CENOE to ensure operational readiness of the
INGD-led early warning platform. This includes the preparation of message templates and pre-registration of phone
numbers of relevant focal points in communities in each of the pilot districts. Furthermore, INGD has established contracts
with radios to ensure early warning can also be disseminated through different radio providers.
For evacuation, WFP has supported INGD to map safe evacuation routes and do community-level evacuation simulations
ahead of the cyclone season.
For anticipatory food assistance, WFP has worked with INGD to map the capacity and prioritization of temporary
accommodation centers in the relevant districts. Furthermore, WFP has mapped and identified retailers that could provide
commodity vouchers in the selected districts.

CERF will disburse funds on a no-regrets basis as soon as the readiness, action or observational trigger is reached. Upon
activation of the first of any trigger, CERF will send out approval letters to the relevant agencies and subsequently will

portion of the funding for early response can only be used if and when an observational trigger is reached. The amount of
funding for readiness vs action vs early response activities will be clearly defined in the pre-approved project proposals and
budgets and communicated in the CERF approval letters. Distinguishing between these cost categories in advance is
important given the different potential scenarios:

Considerations
Both the geographical scope and the impact of cyclones are inherently difficult to predict despite the use of the best available
scientific data. As a result, it is possible that an event causes or exacerbates existing humanitarian needs despite not
formally reaching the thresholds required to utilize the pre-arranged funding. In this case, the RC/HC may request the ERC
to make an allocation through CERF’s Rapid Response window and use the projects already developed for the present
framework, thus reducing the administrative burden for all partners and expediting the release of life-saving funds. This
would not affect the amount of funding pre-arranged for this framework. The same applies to situations in which the
anticipatory action trigger is reached without a subsequent observational trigger.
In general, having an anticipatory action framework supported by CERF neither precludes (nor guarantees) additional CERF
Rapid Response funding to a country for the same or other shocks.

6. Learning
This framework offers significant opportunities to both generate and apply learning, thus contributing to the overall
improvement of humanitarian action in response to sudden-onset shocks. The combination of anticipatory action with early
response or the reliance on mainly in-kind and service provision in the absence of multi-purpose cash assistance are just
some of the features of this framework that may be explored as part of the learning agenda.

Monitoring and evaluation
Each agency will use its existing monitoring & evaluation systems to collect and track data on implementation progress and
outputs achieved. All findings will be shared. In addition, partners have agreed to conduct a joint Post Distribution Monitoring
(PDM) in case of a framework activation. All agencies agreed to share beneficiary data, within existing regulations, and
commit to providing relevant inputs to support the PDM. The final PDM report along with data will be made available to all
framework partners. The PDM will be done in close coordination and communication with participating agencies. In addition,
an after-action review will be conducted to gather lessons learned from the development and potential implementation of
the framework.

7. Validity and future of the framework
The present framework is valid for an initial period of two cyclone seasons (2024/2025 and 2025/2026). The 2024/2025
season will be a pilot phase given that it constitutes the first inter-agency anticipatory action experience for a sudden-onset
shock in Mozambique. In the subsequent 2025/2026 cyclone season, lessons learned from both the development and
potential implementation of the framework will be applied to enhance and increase the scope of the current framework in
collaboration with the national technical working group on anticipatory action. This includes, among other things, potential
changes to the trigger mechanism, included sectors and agencies, financing, co-funding, action plan (especially the
integration of multi-purpose cash), and geographical scope.

8. Annex
Core principles
Each anticipatory action framework is tailored to the local context. OCHA has identified nine core principles that should
apply to the framework in Mozambique:
•

Prioritizing a multi-sectoral approach: Partners should prioritize multi-sectoral, co-targeted interventions, when
possible, to maximize synergies and to achieve great impact.

•

Ensuring the centrality of protection: In line with the IASC centrality of protection policy, and the principle of
“leaving no one behind” (2030 Agenda), all partners implementing anticipatory interventions have the responsibility
to ensure that their response will not aggravate the exposure of communities to risks of violence, insecurity,
extortion and exploitation. Anticipatory actions must also ensure that they benefit all communities (local
communities, displaced communities, returnees, and refugees) based on their needs, with due attention given to
obstacles linked to gender, age, disability or social affiliation.

•

Ensuring gender equality is central to inclusive and accountable anticipatory action: In line with IASC Policy
on Gender Equality and the Empowerment of Women and Girls in Humanitarian Action, all partners are responsible
and accountable to ensure humanitarian action, including anticipatory action is: rights-based; recognizes genderbased and intersectional needs, as well as potential gender-based discrimination in access to resources and
services; facilitate leadership and contribution of crisis-affected women and girls in the planning, implementation,
monitoring and evaluation of anticipatory actions and early response, that directly impact their lives and those of
their dependents and wider communities.

•

Promoting accountability to affected populations and the Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse
(PSEA): Partners engaged in the implementation of the anticipatory action framework are responsible for the
integration of accountability to affected populations approaches in their activities. This includes making all efforts to
solicit, hear, and act upon the voices and priorities of affected people (including the most marginalized and at-risk
women, men, girls and boys) in a coordinated manner, before, during, and after anticipatory action. It also means
ensuring that community feedback leads to corrective action in future anticipatory action.

•

Supporting localization: All partners involved in the implementation of this framework are expected to honor the
HCT’s commitment towards localization. Equitable partnerships with local actors building upon their long-term
relationships and trust with communities during anticipatory action are crucial.

•

Engaging in partnerships: Collaboration across humanitarian actors is key to ensuring that all sectors are
engaged, and that assistance is mobilized according to the AA framework.

•

Generating development and climate adaptation co-benefits: Pilot participants are encouraged to consider how
financing for anticipatory action can complement financing for development by reducing suffering and addressing
the root causes of problems while supporting adaptation to climate change.

•

Capturing learning: Monitoring, evaluation and learning activities should be embedded at every phase of the
development and implementation of the AA framework.

•

Striving for integration: The framework seeks to integrate the anticipatory action activities into the existing
humanitarian architecture and to foster linkages with long term programming, including resilience and development
initiatives.

Trigger Monitoring Methodology
The exposed area is determined based on the forecast using the cone of uncertainty, accounting for the uncertainty in
location, and the wind contours.
•

•
•

Using the uncertainty cone and the forecasted center of the storm, the exposed area is calculated based on the
wind speed at each forecast point and extends inland only as far as the storm’s intensity meets the respective
thresholds. The uncertainty cone is truncated at points where the storm intensity drops below the threshold, with
interpolation between forecast points ensuring a smooth representation of wind speeds. Additional buffers are
applied around the truncated uncertainty cone based on the maximum wind contour for the threshold at each point.
For the action trigger, two thresholds are applied, resulting in two exposed areas: one for severe tropical storms
and another for tropical cyclones.
For the observational trigger, only the tropical cyclone threshold is used, resulting in one exposed area.
