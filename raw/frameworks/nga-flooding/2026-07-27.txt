1. Executive Summary 
Context 
The humanitarian sector is scaling up anticipatory action (AA), enabling proactive mobilization to get ahead of 
predictable climate -related shocks and disease outbreaks through robust forecasting (triggers), pre -agreed 
activities, and pre-arranged funding. This approach has proven effective, dignified, cost -efficient, and protective 
of development gains.  
Nigeria faces recurrent and destructive flooding. In 2025, floods affected more than 400,000 people in 25 States 
across Nigeria, with 232 people losing their lives. In 2024, floods displaced 1.2 million people, destroyed 116,000 
homes, critical infrastructure and 1. 4 million hectares of farmland  (an estimated loss of 1.1 million tons and $1 
billion, enough to feed 13 million people ). This is while people were still reeling from devastating floods i n 2022 
which had displaced 4 million people and destroyed 600,000 hectares of farmland.   
This document present s the collective framework for flood anticipatory action in Nigeria. It entails coordinated 
packages of assistance planned to be delivered to people at risk of riverine floods in Nigeria across seven local 
government areas ( LGAs) – Fufore, Yola South, Yola North, Girei, Demsa, Numan, and Lamurde – along the 
Benue River in Adamawa State , an area prone to recurrent flooding . The framework was developed through a 
participatory process of humanitarian partners  and the Inter -Sector Coordination Group who formed a  Core 
Anticipatory Action Group, coordinated by OCHA, under the overall leadership of the Resident and Humanitarian 
Coordinator (RC/HC) and in collaboration with the government of Nigeria.  
The financing  
This framework pre ‑arranges US $6 million in pooled ‑fund financing – $5 million from the UN’s Central 
Emergency Response Fund (CERF) and $1 million from the Nigeria Humanitarian Fund (NHF).  It aims to protect 
up to 764,864 people in the seven LGAs through timely, targeted multi-sectoral assistance. Payout of the CERF 
funds will only occur if triggers are reached, indicating a major riverine flood. NHF funds, while prioritizing a river 
flooding event, have further flexibility to be reprogrammed to a flash flood scenario or regular rapid response to 
prioritized humanitarian gaps, in case a riverine flood is not predicted.  
CERF funding is pre -arranged for one activation within two years of ERC endorsement, while NHF funding has 
been approved for 2026. Extensions are subject to ERC/advisory board approval. However, this does not 
preclude additional co-financing from the Government of Nigeria, other humanitarian partners, funds and financial 
institutions that may alter the pre -arranged amount for 2026. The World Food Programme , for instance,  has 
committed an additional $2M in co-financing to scale up multi-purpose cash assistance. 
The trigger 
The trigger mechanism for this framework includes two separate triggers which can be activated independently:  
1. Riverine flooding trigger [action and readiness]: used to release CERF and NHF funding to implement 
activities in the 7 LGAs along the Benue River in Adamawa state (Fufore, Yola South, Yola North, Girei, 
Demsa, Numan, and Lamurde). 
2. Flash flooding observational trigger: used to release only NHF funding, as a reprogramming /secondary 
option, to implement activities in the 4 additional flash -flooding LGAs in the BAY states ( Mobbar, 
Maiduguri, Geidam, Jere) 
 
1. Riverine flooding trigger 
The financing and activities in this framework for riverine flooding (Benue River, Adamawa State) are governed 
by a two-stage trigger mechanism consisting of an action trigger for full activation and a readiness trigger for early 
pre-positioning. 
1.1 Readiness Trigger 
The readiness trigger is activated if the GloFAS ensemble mean discharge exceeds 3,132 m³/s at Wuroboki 
(G5004) within the first 12 days of leadtime of the forecast, or if the GloFAS reanalysis exceeds the same 
threshold. The readiness trigger has a return period of approximately 1 -in-3 years and is designed to fire ahead 
of the action trigger, enabling pre -positioning before full act ivation is confirmed. In observed event years it has 
fired up to 10 days ahead of the action trigger.  
As occurred in 2012 and 2018, it is possible that the readiness trigger is not met in advance of the action 
trigger.  3 
1.2 Action Trigger 
The action trigger is activated if at least 6 of 10 selected Google GRRR gauge stations along the Benue 
simultaneously exceed their individual 4 -year return period thresholds (~1,100 –1,117 m³/s per gauge) on the 
same day. These 10 gauges were selected from the full set of 42 available GloFAS and Google gauges, as those 
with the highest performance in detecting historical flood events. The overall return period of the  action trigger is 
approximately 1-in-4.5 years. Operationally, the trigger is expected to fire up to 5 days before a 1 -in-4 year flood 
event.  
 
2. Flash flooding trigger (monitoring only) 
This framework will also monitor  flash flooding to inform reprogramming options for the NHF and other partner -
specific early/rapid response mobilization. It will be triggered if: The flood exposure (calculated by overlaying the 
satellite flood extent with the population distribution) for an LGA exceeds its corresponding threshold, listed below: 
• Mobbar: 14,720 people  
• Maiduguri: 23,740 people 
• Geidam: 5,501 people 
• Jere: 103,612 people 
 
Note that due to biases in the flood extent data between geographies, these values do not correspond directly to 
the number of people impacted and should only be compared to other values from the same LGA.  Furthermore, 
the levels have been set to give an equal probability of triggering in each LGA, with an overall return period of 
approximately 4 years. 
 
The action plan  
The framework will enable humanitarian partners to deliver anticipatory assistance  in a coordinated manner.  
Partners plan to provide multi-purpose cash as well as in-kind assistance and services across agriculture, health, 
water, sanitation and hygiene - WASH, shelter and non-food items, protection and common services – early 
warning and joint targeting.  
To promote choice and dignity among beneficiaries, and in line with the Inter-Sector Coordination Group’s (ISCG) 
shift to cash -based response, the activities in this framework  include various forms of cash assistance. CERF 
funding will be made available to UN agencies while the NHF funding will exclusively cover national NGO partners, 
thus strengthening localization. Other partners are invited and encouraged to scale flood anticipatory assistance 
via geographical alignment, sectoral complementarity, coordinated targeting and  coordinated modal ities of 
assistance. All activities in this framework  are critical and align with the lifesaving criteria of the 202 6 HNRP 
prioritization and maintain CERF and NHF’s lifesaving focus. CERF will provide funding to UN agencies focusing 
on Food Security, WASH, Health and Shelter -NFI, underpinned by multi-purpose cash assistance  (MPCA). 
Meanwhile, NHF funding plans to focus on common services such as early warning, child protection and gender-
based violence (GBV), underpinned by MPCA.  
The pre-agreed actions will focus on the following modalities: 
• Communicating risks, engaging with communities, and early warning messaging:  early warnings, 
community disease surveillance and hygiene messaging. 
• Providing in-kind assistance: agriculture support (including animal feed, vaccination, protective dikes and 
drainage systems), emergency medical, dignity and hygiene supplies, and water infrastructure rehabilitation. 
• Provision of MPCA: Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA), aimed at strengthening household-level flood 
preparedness. 
• Facilitating access to services : through mobile health clinics, temporary sexual and reproductive health 
(SRH) camps, water quality measures, referrals for protection needs, and disease surveillance  and 
prevention services.

Learning 
Based on lessons from past activations, the following changes were made to the present framework iteration: 
1. Moved towards phased triggers: readiness trigger, action trigger: this c reates more lead time for 
verification, procurement, pre-positioning, and community engagement. 
2. CERF envelope was adjusted to better align with operational lessons : Partner allocations adjusted to 
reflect delivery experience, and MPCA requires a modest increase. 
3. Early warning becomes more actionable: Warning is shifting from information -sharing to practical risk 
communication. Communities need clear guidance on what to do, when, where and with whose support. 
4. Improved targeting: targeting will combine social register data, flood exposure, and community validation. 
A dedicated inter-agency targeting process is established. 
5. NHF positioned as complementary to CERF : NHF will use fewer partners and will support readiness, 
targeting, localization, and multi-purpose cash (MPCA). 
 
Validity period 
While the framework itself is not time -bound, the CERF pre -arranged funding, following endorsement by the 
Emergency Relief Coordinator, will be valid for a two-year period (covering the 2026 and 2027 seasons), with the 
possibility of extension. NHF funds are expected to be utilized by the end of 2026.

2. Flooding in Nigeria 
Nigeria is highly prone to climate hazards like floods, storms, ocean surges, droughts and wildfires. Due to climate 
change, flood risks are expected to increase even further through more frequent and intense rainfall. 1 The 2024 
floods inundated 34 of the 36 states, with Borno State being the worst-affected, impacting three million people  
and leading to a loss of nearly 700 lives and further displacing around 896,000 people. Flood water was estimated 
to have destroyed 226,000 homes, critical infrastructure and 1.3 million hectares of farmland, causing the loss of 
1.1 million tons of maize, sorghum and rice  (an estimated $1 billion in crop losses ), enough to feed 13 million 
people. The disaster also precipitated a severe nationwide cholera outbreak . A staggering 46 per cent of IDP 
sites are in congested  flood-prone areas. By mid-October 2024, more than 14,000 suspected cases and 378 
deaths had been recorded, mainly in Borno, Adamawa, Jigawa, Yobe and Kano.  In 2025, floods affected more 
than 400,000 people in 25 States across Nigeria, with 232 people losing their lives .2

3. Coordination Arrangements and Partners’ Own Financing 
A multi-partner Core Anticipatory Action Group, including UN and NGO partners, and members of the Inter-Sector 
Coordination Group (ISCG), coordinated by OCHA, develop ed the framework. The Group has also been 
supporting nationally-led AA work, led by the Office of the Vice -President and co -driven by the Nigeria 
Hydrological Services Agency (NiHSA), Nigeria Metrological Agency (NiMet) and National Emergency 
Management Agency (NEMA). A government -chaired Trigger Task Team, including NiHSA, NiMet, NEMA, the 
National Space Research and Development Agency (NASRDA) and OCHA, review ed relevant trigger sources 
for monitoring and use in the framework. This is supported by essential contributions from government agencies, 
including historical observational and impact data. 
AA is a collective undertaking. Participating partners are committing internal resources for all preparedness 
activities and leveraging their economies of scale in terms of staff, warehousing and other supply chain capacities, 
on the backs of which anticipatory assistance will be delivered. In addition, WFP has matched the US$2 million 
allocated to it for multipurpose cash assistance, further strengthening the resources available for early action.   
4. Synergies with Strategic Humanitarian Priorities and Other Initiatives  
This framework aligns with the Government of Nigeria ’s leadership on anticipatory action.  Following extensive 
work and multi-day high-level national strategic and technical workshops, led by the Office of the Vice President 
of Nigeria, the Government is preparing to invest up to $60 million towards anticipatory action and has requested 
OCHA’s technical, planning, and coordination support and coordination and contribution of relevant humanitarian 
partners. The initial focus is on riverine flooding, where forecasting allows sufficient lead time for anticipatory 
assistance and reliable historical data is available from national and global sources. The investment is initially 
expected to target 14 states along the Benue and Niger rivers. In parallel, 4 to 5 additional states will be selected 
to pilot anticipatory action for flash flooding, helping to test and refine approaches for faster -onset flood risks. 
 
The Government of Nigeria’s planned investment demonstrates proactive humanitarian leadership, establishing 
a regional benchmark for anticipatory disaster response.  This commitment was catalyzed by humanitarian 
partners’ sustained practical advocacy, including facilitating national dialogues, embedding AA in strategic

This framework further draws on the HNRP Strategy for 2024-2025, which outlined steps towards reforming the 
humanitarian response to achieve greater efficiency.  A third element of reform is moving from a reactive to  a 
proactive response, through anticipatory action to  humanitarian events. Five per cent of the programmed  
resources in the 2026 HNRP are intended to address cyclical events such as floods. This is a pioneering step in 
humanitarian response planning where AA resource needs for predictable shocks are articulated at the strategic 
planning moment, while inter-sector priorities articulate at-risk areas; project and quantify the impact; identify the 
time whe n risks converge; and outline appropriate modalities and sector-specific activities to address these 
risks/future shocks.

The framework draws on partners’ previous anticipatory action investments, pilots and programmes. Specifically, 
it draws on lessons from the 2025 framework activation.  The activation in Adamawa State demonstrated that 
Nigeria’s anticipatory action system can successfully translate forecast information into early humanitarian action 
when triggers, financing, coordination, and implementation mechanisms are aligned. Early w arning messages 
reached communities ahead of peak flooding, and multipurpose cash assistance was delivered rapidly following 
trigger activation, contributing to improved food security, reduced use of negative coping strategies, and greater 
household resilience during the flood period. The activation also highlighted several areas for improvement. While 
institutional stakeholders generally viewed the trigger and activation process as timely, many community 
members perceived assistance as arriving too close t o, or after, the onset of flood impacts, particularly in areas 
affected by rapid-onset flash flooding. Key lessons included the need to strengthen trigger sensitivity and flexibility 
through phased or multi -layered activation approaches, improve targeting systems and social registry data to 
reduce exclusion errors, expand community -centred early warning and risk communication, and increase 
preparedness investments such as pre-positioning, beneficiary verification, and readiness financing.  
 
The framework also builds on the NHF allocation in 2024 ($2m), which was based on a no -regrets strategy for 
readiness, anticipatory action and early response for riverine floods, flash floods and cholera risks in two of the 
774 LGAs in Nigeria – Bama (Borno) and Numan (Adamawa). Approximately 276,600 people were estimated to 
be exposed to the aforementioned risks. While the allocation process was underway, the large -scale floods, 
associated with dam-break, occurred and the proactive allocation served as a catalyst for early response.  
 
The framework builds on Nigeria’s expanding anticipatory action ecosystem, including partner -led initiatives by 
NRCS/IFRC, Save the Children, IRC, Welthungerhilfe/Oxfam through the WAHAFA Facility, and others. For this 
framework iteration, the focus is on stronger alignment and complementarity across these efforts.  
8 
Where partners have existing protocols or trigger -based plans, the framework will seek to align forecasting 
sources, trigger monitoring, early warning, targeting, implementation timelines and delivery modalities. Global 
tools such as GloFAS will be used al ongside national data and validation from NiHSA and NiMet, helping to 
strengthen consistency between technical analysis and operational decision-making. 
The framework does not replace partner protocols. It provides a common coordination platform to support a more 
coherent, government -led approach, especially in riverine flood -risk areas along the Benue and Niger river 
systems and in selected locations where flash-flood approaches will be piloted. 
This framework’s priority is to reduce fragmentation and ensure partners align around common risk analysis, 
agreed triggers, harmonised targeting, coordinated early warning, centralised feedback and clear geographic and 
sectoral responsibilities. Partners will also leverage their own preparedness investments, staff presence, 
warehousing, supply chains and community networks to support faster and more coherent anticipatory action 
once triggers are reached.

Geographical Focus 
Riverine Floods: 
The geographical selection for the AA framework followed a four-tiered approach:  
1) analysis of historic data of observed floods and impacts (based on NiHSA and NiMet data).  
2) analysis of historical flood risk (based on NEMA data). 
3) analysis of historic average population exposed to floods (based on FloodScan); and  
4) severity of needs based on the 2025 HNRP People in Need (PiN) figures 
Based on this analysis and consultations with  humanitarian and Government partners and an evaluation of 
achievable AA investments with aim to maximize impact of the limited AA pre -arranged funding, AA Group 
partners have prioritized 7 LGAs in Adamawa State prone to riverine flooding – Fufore, Yola South, Yola North, 
Girei, Demsa, Numan, and Lamurde (in blue on the map).

Household Targeting 
Core AA Group partners agreed to apply a coordinated and transparent household targeting approach that 
combines geographic flood -risk exposure, vulnerability data from the National Social Registry (NSR), 
household vulnerability with community -based validation  to ensure that assistance reaches households 
most at risk and least able to prepare, relocate or recover. Building on the AAR and PDM lessons from last year’s 
implementation, the framework will place stronger emphasis on early pre -identification, geograph ic de -
duplication, harmonised MPCA transfer values, and clear partner coordination to avoid overlap while improving 
timeliness and accountability. The NSR, managed through NASSCO, provides a strong basis for identifying poor 
and vulnerable households, using geographic targeting, commu nity ranking, community -based targeting and 
proxy means testing, with profiles covering approximately 19 million vulnerable individuals across indicators such 
as income, food consumption, livelihoods, coping capacity, family composition and other vulnerability categories. 
Data-sharing arrangements with NASSCO have already been agreed, and a coordinated targeting validation 
exercise will be undertaken to confirm inclusion and exclusion, verify household vulnerability through community 
structures, align partner caseloads and ensure that WFP, IOM and LESGO use the common registry, harmonised 
transfer values and geographic de -duplication before activation. This approach is consistent with the revised 
framework’s stronger focus on timely, flexible and people -centred early action, particularly through MPCA, while 
maintaining complementarity with livelihoods, health, WASH/NFI and relocation-site preparedness interventions.

6. Planned Activities 
Selection criteria 
Anticipatory actions aim to protect those most at risk before a shock fully unfolds. Actions selected followed these 
criteria: 
• Anticipatory character: Activities effectively prevent or reduce the humanitarian impact of floods. 
• Feasible within the window of opportunity:  activities can be delivered within the lead time – i.e. between 
the trigger and the peak impact of flood waters. 
• Operational capacity: Partners have the necessary institutional (thematic, logistic, administrative, financial, 
human resources) capacity to implement these activities at scale within the lead time . 
• No regrets approach: In case of a false alarm, proposed actions benefit (and do not negatively affect) people 
targeted. 
Objectives 
The pre-agreed activities aim to mitigate the main humanitarian impacts of floods. They focus on: 
• Enabling communities to take informed decisions 
• Mitigating loss of lives and household assets 
• Safeguarding agricultural production and reducing yield losses 
• Mitigating displacement  
• Avoiding deteriorating food security and other needs 
• Ensuring the availability of safe water for human and animal consumption 
• Reducing the risk of disease outbreaks, particularly cholera 
• Reducing morbidity and mortality through access to essential goods and services 
• Mitigating flood- and displacement-related protection risks (including GBV, family separation, loss of 
documentation, etc.) and use of negative coping strategies

Lessons learned 19 
Based on lessons learned from the 2025 framework activation, partners have made the following adjustments to 
their programming: 
To reduce timing gaps, WFP will prioritise a two-tier activation model, with a preparedness trigger releasing funds 
for early readiness activities and an action trigger initiating full interventions. This is intended to allow sectoral 
actions to be implemented within the same operational window. 
FAO will improve targeting through updated vulnerability maps and community validation, responding to gaps in 
beneficiary identification and awareness. It will also strengthen veterinary preparedness by investing in cold-chain 
and solar-powered equipment, linking animal-health data to community early -warning systems, and improving 
coordination with waste-management authorities following drainage works. 
WHO will planning, procurement and pre -positioning, using strategic LGA hubs, real -time logistics tracking and 
contingency procurement to reduce supply delays and stock -outs. It will further institutionalise PHEOC -led 
multisectoral coordination, joint supervision and integrated monitoring, while embedding anticipatory interventions 
in routine primary healthcare outreach and state emergency-preparedness plans 
Building on integrated WASH, health, nutrition and risk-communication delivery, UNICEF is expected to prioritise 
earlier pre-positioning of essential supplies and tighter joint planning, supervision and monitoring with government 
and health partners. It should also sustain local -language, two -way community engagement and accessible 
feedback mechanisms to strengthen preparedness, inclusion and accountability. 
Joint Approach to Targeting and to Early Warning 
AA partners agreed to apply a coordinated approach that links joint household targeting with harmonised  early 
warning and community engagement. To ensure consistency, avoid mixed messages and support timely 
anticipatory action, a single agency will disseminate early warning messages on behalf of all implementing 
partners. Given its community presence, acces s and geographic reach, the Nigerian Red Cross Society , 
supported through NHF funding, will lead early warning messaging and dissemination for AA partners. This will 
be done in close coordination with the National Orientation Agency, which will support Government -led public 
communication through established channels, including radio, television and other community outreach platforms. 
This approach reflects lessons from last year’s implementation by strengthening coordination, ensuring 
communities receive clear and trusted messages, and linking early warning di rectly to targeting, preparedness 
and trigger-based assistance. 
7. Pre-agreed Financing 
Complementarity between pooled funds 
CERF and NHF funding will be complementary.  
1. CERF will provide funding to UN agencies focusing on Food Security  (incl. agriculture), WASH, Health and 
Shelter-NFI, underpinned by MPCA.  
2. NHF funding will focus on common services such as early warning  and joint targeting, child protection and 
GBV, underpinned by MPCA. In case the primary riverine flooding trigger does not get reached (Scenario 1), 
resources may be pivoted to flash floods (Scenario  2) or broader reprogramming (Scenario 3). Furthermore, 
the NHF will leverage its comparative advantage to have an exclusive partnership with national NGOs.  
Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF) 
For this anticipatory action framework  in Nigeria, the ERC has pre-agreed to allocate up to $5 million from the 
CERF only if triggers for riverine flooding are reached within the specified season and duration.  
CERF will disburse funds once three conditions are met:  
• Endorsement by the Humanitarian Coordinator of the framework and the CERF application package , 
including agency project proposals and budgets; and  
• Endorsement by the Emergency Relief Coordinator of the finalized framework, as well as the CERF agency 
project proposals and budgets; and  
• Crossing trigger thresholds of the framework once fully approved and operational. 
CERF will disburse funds on a no -regrets basis as soon as either the readiness trigge r or the action trigger is 
reached. While a portion of the fund s can be used immediately upon disbursement for readiness activities, the 
remaining portion of the funding can only be used if and when an action trigger is met. The amount of funding for 
readiness vs action activities will be clearly defined in the pre-approved project proposals and budgets. The need 
to distinguish between these two cost categories in advance is important given the different potential scenarios:  
• Scenario A: all-systems go (readiness trigger followed a few days later by an action trigger)  
• Scenario B: false alarm (readiness trigger, but no action trigger)  20 
• Scenario B-2: false alarm, followed by readiness + action trigger within a maximum of 6 months  
• Scenario C: action trigger, but no readiness trigger.   
• Scenario D: no activation at all  
 
Upon activation of the framework, CERF will send out approval letters to the relevant agencies and subsequently 
will disburse all the funds6. If the trigger thresholds are not crossed, the pre-arranged funds cannot be transferred 
for other purposes. The funds remain pre -arranged for the following year (up until the end of the 2 -year validity 
period of the pre-arranged funding ). Further details can be found in the CERF project documents and CERF 
communications. The CERF secretariat must approve any changes to the projects and budgets  as per usual 
practice. 
Note: Having an anticipatory action framework supported by CERF does not preclude the RC/HC or HCT from 
seeking additional CERF Rapid Response funding for the same or other shocks that may be experienced. At the 
same time, it does not guarantee it either.

8. Learning, Monitoring and evaluation 
This framework offers significant opportunities to both generate and apply learning, thus contributing to the overall 
improvement of humanitarian action in response to sudden-onset shocks.  
Each agency will use its existing monitoring & evaluation systems to collect and track data on implementation 
progress and outputs achieved. All reports and findings will be shared. In case a joint evaluation is conducted, all 
UN agencies receiving CERF funding agreed to share data  on targeted people, within existing regulations, and 
committed to providing relevant inputs to support the evaluation. Furthermore, it was agreed that lessons learning 
will be facilitated through after action or peer reviews, with findings shared and discussed among  stakeholders 
involved. 
Based on lessons from past activations, the following changes were made to the present framework iteration : 
1. Moved towards phased triggers: readiness trigger, action trigger : this c reates more lead time for 
verification, procurement, pre-positioning, and community engagement. 
2. CERF envelope was adjusted to better align with operational lessons: Partner allocations adjusted to 
reflect delivery experience, and MPCA requires a modest increase. 
3. Early warning becomes more actionable: Warning is shifting from information -sharing to practical risk 
communication. Communities need clear guidance on what to do, when, where and with whose support. 
4. Improved targeting: targeting will combine social register data, flood exposure, and community validation. 
A dedicated inter-agency targeting process is established. 
5. NHF positioned as complementary to CERF : NHF will use fewer partners and will support  readiness, 
targeting, localization, and multi-purpose cash (MPCA).

Core principles 
Each anticipatory action framework is tailored to the local context. OCHA has identified nine core principles that 
should apply to the framework in Nigeria: 
• Prioritizing a multi -sectoral approach:  Partners should prioritize multi-sectoral, co -targeted 
interventions, when possible, to maximize synergies and to achieve great impact.  
• Ensuring the centrality of protection:  In line with the IASC centrality of protection policy, and the 
principle of “leaving no one behind” (2030 Agenda), all partners implementing anticipatory interventions 
have the responsibility to ensure that their response will not aggravate the exposure o f communities to 
risks of violence, insecurity, extortion and exploitation. Anticipatory actions must also ensure that they 
benefit all communities (local communities, displaced communities, returnees, and refugees) based on 
their needs, with due attention given to obstacles linked to gender, age, disability or social affiliation.  
• Ensuring gender equality is central to inclusive and accountable anticipatory action:  In line with 
IASC Policy on Gender Equality and the Empowerment of Women and Girls in Humanitarian Action, all 
partners are responsible and accountable to ensure humanitarian action, including anticipatory action is: 
rights-based; recognizes gender-based and intersectional needs, as well as potential gender -based 
discrimination in access to resources and services; facilitate leadership and contribution of crisis-affected 
women and girls in the planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluati on of anticipatory actions and 
early response, that directly impact their lives and those of their dependents and wider communities.  
• Promoting accountability to affected populations and the Protection from Sexual Exploitation and 
Abuse (PSEA): Partners engaged in the implementation of the anticipatory framework are responsible 
for the integration of accountability to affected populations approaches in their activities. This includes 
making all efforts to solicit, hear, and act upon the voices and priorities of affected people (including the 
most marginalized and at-risk women, men, girls and boys) in a coordinated manner, before, during, and 
after anticipatory action. It also means ensuring that community feedback leads to corrective action in 
future anticipatory action. 
• Supporting localization: All partners involved in the implementation of this framework are expected to 
honor HCT’s commitment towards localization. Equitable partnerships with local actors building upon 
their long-term relationships and trust with communities during anticipatory action are crucial.  
25 
• Engaging in partnerships: Collaboration across humanitarian actors is key to ensuring that all sectors 
are engaged, and that assistance is mobilized according to the AA framework. 
• Generating development and climate adaptation co -benefits: Framework partners are encouraged 
to consider how financing for anticipatory action can complement financing for development by reducing 
suffering and addressing the root causes of problems while supporting adaptation to climate change.  
• Capturing learning: Monitoring, evaluation and learning activities should be embedded at every phase 
of the development and implementation of the AA framework. 
• Striving for integration:  The framework seeks to integrate the anticipatory action activities into the 
existing humanitarian architecture and to foster linkages with long term programming, including resilience 
and development initiatives.
